Last Friday, November 13, 2026, the world population was supposed to become infinite.

It's not a joke.
It's the title of a provocative article published in Science on November 4, 1960: "Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026".

Heinz von Foerster and two colleagues had taken the historical population data, found the curve that described it best and extended it.
The doubling time shrank until it reached zero on exactly that day.

What happened?
Already from the 1960s growth stopped following that curve and today people talk about stabilization by the end of the century.
The math was right. It was the world that disagreed.

I wanted to share it because it's curious, but also because it made me think of the charts on AI that I often see here on LinkedIn.
Like the METR one on tasks doubling in duration roughly every 7 months, with the line extended forward to AGI.

A curve describes the past, but doesn't guarantee the future.
- the data to train models on isn't infinite
- energy and datacenters cost more and more
- every extra point on the benchmarks costs more than the previous one

And whoever has the most interest in extending that line is often whoever needs to close the next funding round.

PS: November 13 was also von Foerster's birthday. Maybe he was joking a little too.
